    \node[st, fill=darkcyan!15] (pkt) at (3.6,0) {Packets\\ (RTP)};
    \node[st, fill=darkcyan!25] (net) at (5.4,0) {Network};
    \node[st, fill=darkcyan!15] (buf) at (7.2,0) {Buffer};
    \node[st, fill=cardinalred!12] (dec) at (9.0,0) {Decoder};
    \node[st] (mod) at (9.0,-1.5) {Model or\\ screen};
    \draw[->] (cam) -- (enc);
    \draw[->] (enc) -- (pkt);
    \draw[->] (pkt) -- (net);
    \draw[->] (net) -- (buf);
    \draw[->] (buf) -- (dec);
    \draw[->] (dec) -- (mod);
    \draw[coolgray] (-0.7,0.75) -- (3.6,0.75)
      node[midway, above] {camera node};
    \draw[coolgray] (6.5,0.75) -- (9.7,0.75)
      node[midway, above] {reader node};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \par\endgroup
  \vskip 0.5em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A camera makes a frame each 33 ms at 30 frames each second. The
          network cannot carry the raw frames.
    \item The encoder makes the frames small. The protocol cuts them into
          packets and gives each packet a time.
    \item The reader collects the packets, waits for missing ones, and
          decodes the frame for the model.
    \item Each step adds delay. Part 3 measures the sum.
  \end{itemize}
  \keypoint{This part: how large a stream is, how an encoder makes it small,
    and how RTSP, RTP, and RTCP carry it.}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ raw rates
\begin{frame}{Resolution, frame rate, and bit rate}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Resolution}: the pixels of one frame, width $\times$
          height.
    \item \textbf{Frame rate}: the frames each second (fps).
    \item \textbf{Bit rate}: the bits each second of the stream, in Mbit/s.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 0.2em
  Raw video at 30 frames each second:
  \vskip 0.2em
  \footnotesize
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.12}
  \setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Resolution} & \textbf{RGB frame} & \textbf{RGB stream} &
    \textbf{YUV 4:2:0 frame} & \textbf{YUV 4:2:0 stream} \\
    \midrule
    640 $\times$ 360   & 675 KB  & 165.9 Mbit/s  & 338 KB  & 82.9 Mbit/s \\
    1280 $\times$ 720  & 2700 KB & 663.6 Mbit/s  & 1350 KB & 331.8 Mbit/s \\
    1920 $\times$ 1080 & 6075 KB & 1493.0 Mbit/s & 3038 KB & 746.5 Mbit/s \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.5em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Bit rate = bytes of one frame $\times$ 8 $\times$ frames each
          second. 1280 $\times$ 720 $\times$ 3 $\times$ 8 $\times$ 30 =
          663.6 Mbit/s.
    \item The XIAO has a Wi-Fi link of at most 150 Mbit/s. One Wi-Fi
          channel serves all groups.
    \item A stream for the network must be 100 times smaller or more. The
          encoder does this.
  \end{itemize}
\end{frame}

        } }

        \draw[coolgray, fill=darkcyan!25] (2.2,0.4) rectangle ++(0.75,0.75);
        \node at (2.575,0.775) {U};
        \draw[coolgray, fill=cardinalred!20] (3.1,0.4) rectangle ++(0.75,0.75);
        \node at (3.475,0.775) {V};
        \node[below, align=center] at (3.025,0.35) {2 colour values\\ for
          the 4 pixels};
      \end{tikzpicture}
      \vskip 0.4em
      {\footnotesize 4 pixels: 4 + 1 + 1 = 6 bytes, so 1.5 bytes for each
      pixel. RGB needs 3.\par}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.54\textwidth}
      \small
      \begin{itemize}
        \item Y is the brightness. U and V are the colour.
        \item The eye sees fine detail in the brightness, not in the
              colour. So one U and one V serve a square of 4 pixels.
        \item Video encoders work on YUV 4:2:0. This halves the raw size
              before the compression starts.
        \item A model needs RGB. The reader converts each decoded frame.
              This costs time on the board.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ MJPEG
\begin{frame}{MJPEG: one JPEG image for each frame}
  \begingroup\centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\scriptsize, >=stealth,
      st/.style={draw=coolgray, rounded corners=2pt, fill=boxgray,
                 align=center, text width=1.8cm, minimum height=0.85cm}]
    \node[st] (a) at (0,0) {Blocks of\\ 8 $\times$ 8 pixels};
    \node[st] (b) at (2.5,0) {Frequencies\\ (DCT)};
    \node[st, fill=cardinalred!12] (c) at (5.0,0) {Round the\\ fine detail};
    \node[st] (d) at (7.5,0) {Short codes\\ for the values};
    \draw[->] (a) -- (b); \draw[->] (b) -- (c); \draw[->] (c) -- (d);
    \node[cardinalred, below=0.05cm of c] {quality setting};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \par\endgroup
  \vskip 0.4em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Motion JPEG compresses each frame alone, as a photo. The stream is
          a series of JPEG files.
    \item A lost frame damages only that frame. Each frame is a start
          point, so a new reader shows an image at once.
    \item The coding needs little computation. The camera module of the
          XIAO makes the JPEG itself: the stream sketch reads
          \code{PIXFORMAT\_JPEG} frames.
    \item The quality setting selects how much detail the rounding removes.
          On the XIAO camera, \code{jpeg\_quality} goes from 0 to 63, and a
          lower value gives a better image and a larger file. The sketch
          uses 12.
    \item The cost: the stream sends the same background again in each
          frame.
  \end{itemize}
  \source{Stream sketch \texttt{Streeming\_Video.ino} of the repository
    XIAO-ESP32S3-Sense.}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ H.264
\begin{frame}{H.264: send the changes}
  \begingroup\centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\scriptsize, >=stealth, x=0.82cm]
    \foreach \i/\t in {0/I,1/P,2/P,3/P,4/P,5/P,6/I,7/P,8/P} {
      \ifnum\i=0 \def\c{cardinalred!30}\else\ifnum\i=6
        \def\c{cardinalred!30}\else\def\c{darkcyan!20}\fi\fi
      \draw[coolgray, fill=\c] (\i,0) rectangle ++(0.7,0.7);
      \node at (\i+0.35,0.35) {\t};
    }
    \foreach \i in {1,...,5,7,8} {
      \draw[->, coolgray] (\i-0.3,0.85) to[bend left=50] (\i+0.35,0.85);
    }
    \node[anchor=west] at (9.0,0.35) {\dots};
    \draw[<->, coolgray] (0,-0.25) -- node[below] {group of pictures (GOP):
      from one keyframe to the next} (6,-0.25);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \par\endgroup
  \vskip 0.3em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{I-frame} (keyframe): coded alone, like a JPEG. A decoder
          can start here.
    \item \textbf{P-frame}: for each block, a motion vector to a similar
          block of the frame before, and the small difference.
    \item \textbf{B-frame}: also uses a later frame. The encoder must wait
          for that frame, so B-frames add delay. A live stream uses none:
          the option \code{zerolatency} of x264 and \code{--low-latency}
          of \code{rpicam-vid}.
    \item A lost packet damages its frame and all later frames that copy
          from it, until the next keyframe.
  \end{itemize}
  \source{Raspberry Pi documentation, ``Camera software'', section
    ``Streaming''.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Measured: MJPEG against H.264}
  \small
  One test video, 1280 $\times$ 720, 30 frames each second, 10 s:
  \vskip 0.3em
  \footnotesize
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.26\textwidth}rrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{Moving camera}} &
      \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{Fixed camera}} \\
    \textbf{Format} & \textbf{Mbit/s} & \textbf{PSNR} & \textbf{Mbit/s} &
      \textbf{PSNR} \\
    \midrule
    Raw YUV 4:2:0          & 331.8 & & 331.8 & \\
    MJPEG, quality 5       & 11.09 & 42.0 dB & 10.23 & 41.3 dB \\
    H.264, keyframe 1 s & 2.62 & 41.3 dB & 0.97 & 40.5 dB \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.1em
  {\scriptsize PSNR: the similarity to the original frames; a higher value
  is better. Settings: \texttt{-q:v 5}; x264 veryfast,
  zerolatency, CRF 23.\par}
  \vskip 0.4em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The two formats give almost the same image quality.
    \item Moving camera: H.264 needs 4.2 times fewer bits than MJPEG. Fixed
          camera: 10.5 times fewer. The fixed scene changes only by noise.
    \item MJPEG has almost the same bit rate for the two scenes. It does not
          use the frames before.
    \item Compression against raw YUV: 30 to 32 times for MJPEG, 127 to 341
          times for H.264.
  \end{itemize}
  \source{Experiment of this course: FFmpeg 4.4, x86 work computer.\\
    Video of the repository XIAO-ESP32S3-Sense.}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ I and P
\begin{frame}{Measured: keyframes and predicted frames}
  \small
  Mean size of a frame, 1280 $\times$ 720, keyframe each 30 frames:
  \vskip 0.3em
  \footnotesize
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Scene} & \textbf{I-frame} & \textbf{P-frame} & \textbf{I / P} \\
    \midrule
    Moving camera & 38.4 KB & 9.7 KB & 4.0 \\
    Fixed camera  & 78.8 KB & 1.4 KB & 56.9 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.5em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A fixed camera gives almost empty P-frames. Most of its bits are
          in the keyframes.
    \item The encoder gives a keyframe more bits when the next frames copy
          from it. With only keyframes, a frame of the fixed scene has 19.8
          KB.
    \item A keyframe is a burst. At 2 Mbit/s, the keyframe of the fixed
          scene (64.1 KB) needs 0.26 s on the link: almost 8 frame times.
    \item The network sees a peak each second. A link with only the mean
          bit rate delays each keyframe.
  \end{itemize}
  \source{Experiment of this course: FFmpeg 4.4, x86 work computer.\\
    0.26 s = 65\,631 bytes $\times$ 8 / 2 Mbit/s.}
\end{frame}

  \footnotesize
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.32\textwidth}rrr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Keyframe} & \textbf{Each frame} & \textbf{Each 1 s} &
    \textbf{Each 10 s} \\
    \midrule
    Moving camera, Mbit/s & 5.27 & 2.62 & 2.46 \\
    Fixed camera, Mbit/s  & 4.86 & 0.97 & 0.35 \\
    Wait of a new reader for the first keyframe (median of 10) & 0 & 0.50 s
      & 5.51 s \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.1em
  {\scriptsize 1280 $\times$ 720, x264 CRF 23. The wait: 10 readers of an
  RTSP stream at 2 Mbit/s, each started at a random time.\par}
  \vskip 0.5em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A short interval costs bits. A long one saves bits, mostly for a
          fixed camera.
    \item A new reader can decode only from a keyframe. With a keyframe
          each 10 s, it waits up to 10 s for the first image (here at most
          7.40 s).
    \item After a lost packet, the image has errors until the next
          keyframe.
    \item The lab uses one keyframe each second
          (\code{rpiCameraIDRPeriod: 30}).
  \end{itemize}
  \source{Experiment of this course: FFmpeg 4.4 and MediaMTX v1.21.1 on the
    x86 work computer.}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ rate control
\begin{frame}{Constant quality or constant bit rate}
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Constant quality} (x264: CRF): each frame gets the bits
          that it needs. The bit rate follows the scene.
    \item \textbf{Bit rate limit}: the encoder keeps the stream at a set
          value and changes the quality. A network can plan with it.
  \end{itemize}
  \vskip 0.2em
  Measured, 1280 $\times$ 720, keyframe each 1 s:
  \vskip 0.2em
  \footnotesize
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}lrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    & \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{CRF 23}} &
      \multicolumn{2}{c}{\textbf{Limit 2 Mbit/s}} \\
    \textbf{Scene} & \textbf{Mbit/s} & \textbf{PSNR} & \textbf{Mbit/s} &
      \textbf{PSNR} \\
    \midrule
    Moving camera & 2.62 & 41.3 dB & 2.01 & 40.7 dB \\
    Fixed camera  & 0.97 & 40.5 dB & 2.08 & 40.6 dB \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.4em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Moving camera: the limit removes 23 percent of the bits and
          0.7 dB of quality.
    \item Fixed camera: the limit sends 2.1 times the bits for the same
          quality.
    \item The lab starts with 2 Mbit/s (\code{rpiCameraBitrate}). Part C
          of the lab changes it.
  \end{itemize}
  \source{Experiment of this course: FFmpeg 4.4 on the x86 work computer.}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ RTP header
\begin{frame}{RTP: packets with a time stamp}
  \begingroup\centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\scriptsize, x=0.3cm, y=0.6cm]
    \foreach \x/\w/\t/\c in {0/2/V/boxgray, 2/1/P/boxgray, 3/1/X/boxgray,
        4/4/CC/boxgray, 8/1/M/cardinalred!20, 9/7/PT/darkcyan!20,
        16/16/sequence number/darkcyan!30} {
      \draw[coolgray, fill=\c] (\x,0) rectangle ++(\w,1);
      \node at (\x+\w/2,0.5) {\t};
    }
    \draw[coolgray, fill=cardinalred!20] (0,-1) rectangle ++(32,1);
    \node at (16,-0.5) {timestamp (32 bits)};
    \draw[coolgray, fill=boxgray] (0,-2) rectangle ++(32,1);
    \node at (16,-1.5) {SSRC: the source of the stream (32 bits)};
    \draw[coolgray, fill=white] (0,-3) rectangle ++(32,1);
    \node at (16,-2.5) {payload: a part of an H.264 frame};
    \node[anchor=west] at (32.3,0.5) {4 bytes};
    \node[anchor=west] at (32.3,-0.5) {4 bytes};
    \node[anchor=west] at (32.3,-1.5) {4 bytes};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \par\endgroup
  \vskip 0.4em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A header of 12 bytes. RTP runs on UDP, or inside a TCP
          connection.
    \item \textbf{Sequence number}: plus 1 for each packet. The reader
          finds lost packets and puts packets in order.
    \item \textbf{Timestamp}: the capture time of the frame. Video uses a
          clock of 90\,000 steps each second: 3000 steps for each frame at
          30 frames each second.
    \item \textbf{M} (marker): the last packet of a frame. \textbf{PT}: the
          payload type; the session description gives its codec.
  \end{itemize}
  \source{RFC 3550 (RTP), RFC 6184 (H.264 in RTP).}
\end{frame}

  \begin{itemize}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ RTSP
\begin{frame}{RTSP: the remote control of a stream}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\scriptsize, >=stealth, y=0.7cm]
        \node[font=\footnotesize\bfseries] at (0,0) {Reader};
        \node[font=\footnotesize\bfseries] at (3.6,0) {Server};
        \draw[coolgray] (0,-0.3) -- (0,-6.5);
        \draw[coolgray] (3.6,-0.3) -- (3.6,-6.5);
        \foreach \y/\t/\a in {0.7/OPTIONS/methods, 1.9/DESCRIBE/SDP: the codec,
            3.1/SETUP/session and transport, 4.3/PLAY/200 OK} {
          \draw[->, darkcyan] (0,-\y) -- node[above] {\t} (3.6,-\y-0.15);
          \draw[->, coolgray] (3.6,-\y-0.55) -- node[above] {\a} (0,-\y-0.7);
        }
        \draw[->, cardinalred, line width=1.2pt] (3.6,-5.5) --
          node[above] {RTP and RTCP} (0,-5.65);
        \draw[->, darkcyan] (0,-6.15) -- node[above] {TEARDOWN} (3.6,-6.3);
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \small
      \begin{itemize}
        \item A text protocol like HTTP, on one TCP connection. Port 554
              by default; MediaMTX uses 8554.
        \item DESCRIBE returns the session description (SDP): the codec,
              the clock, the tracks.
        \item SETUP selects the transport: RTP on UDP, or inside this TCP
              connection.
        \item PLAY starts the stream. TEARDOWN ends it.
        \item Cameras and MediaMTX use RTSP 1.0 (RFC 2326). RFC 7826 is
              version 2.0.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \source{RFC 2326 (RTSP 1.0), RFC 7826 (RTSP 2.0), RFC 8866 (SDP).}
\end{frame}

% ------------------------------------------------------------------ RTCP
\begin{frame}{RTCP: reports about the stream}
  \begingroup\centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\scriptsize, >=stealth,
      dev/.style={draw=coolgray, rounded corners=2pt, fill=boxgray,
                  align=center, text width=1.8cm, minimum height=0.8cm}]
    \node[dev] (s) at (0,0) {Sender};
    \node[dev] (r) at (7.6,0) {Reader};
    \draw[->, cardinalred] ([yshift=4pt]s.east) -- node[above]
      {Sender report: clock time = RTP timestamp} ([yshift=4pt]r.west);
    \draw[->, darkcyan] ([yshift=-4pt]r.west) -- node[below]
      {Receiver report: lost packets, jitter} ([yshift=-4pt]s.east);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \par\endgroup
  \vskip 0.5em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item RTCP runs next to RTP: on the next UDP port, or on the next
          channel of the RTSP connection.
    \item The \textbf{sender report} links the RTP timestamp to the clock
          time of the sender. The reader can then find the capture time of
          a frame and synchronize audio and video.
    \item The \textbf{receiver report} gives the part of the packets that
          were lost and the jitter. A sender can lower its bit rate.
    \item RTCP uses at most 5 percent of the bandwidth of the session.
    \item In the experiment, the first sender report came 0.08 s after
          PLAY: 25 packets, 33\,204 bytes sent.
  \end{itemize}
  \source{RFC 3550, sections 6.2 and 6.4. Experiment of this course:
    MediaMTX v1.21.1.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RTP over UDP or over TCP}
  \footnotesize
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.18}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.18\textwidth}L{0.36\textwidth}L{0.38\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    & \textbf{UDP} & \textbf{TCP (interleaved)} \\
    \midrule
    Ports   & Two more ports: 8000 (RTP) and 8001 (RTCP) on the server &
              The RTSP connection only \\
    Loss    & A lost packet stays lost: the image has errors until the next
              keyframe & TCP sends the packet again: no image errors \\
    Delay   & Low and stable & Grows on a bad link: later packets wait for
              the resend \\
    Network & A firewall or a router can block it & Passes where the RTSP
              connection passes \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.5em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The MediaMTX documentation: UDP is ``the most performant'', TCP
          ``the most versatile''.
    \item MediaMTX drops packets for a reader that is too slow. A larger
          write queue keeps more packets and uses more RAM.
    \item The lab uses TCP first. Part C of the lab tries UDP and compares
          the delay.
  \end{itemize}
  \source{MediaMTX documentation: ``RTSP-specific features'',\\
    ``Decrease packet loss''.}
\end{frame}

\exerciseframe{bit rate of two streams}{%
  \small
  Two streams of one group. The sizes are example values, near the values
  of the experiment.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item The XIAO sends MJPEG: 640 $\times$ 480, 10 frames each second, a
          mean JPEG of 24 KB.
    \item The Raspberry Pi sends H.264: 1280 $\times$ 720, 30 frames each
          second, one keyframe each second. A keyframe has 40 KB, a P-frame
          9 KB.
  \end{enumerate}
  Calculate:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[a.] the bit rate of each stream in Mbit/s (1 KB = 1024 bytes);
    \item[b.] the compression factor of the H.264 stream against raw YUV
          4:2:0 (331.8 Mbit/s);
    \item[c.] the total of 12 groups. Does it fit a Wi-Fi channel that
          carries 30 Mbit/s?
    \item[d.] the time of one keyframe on a link of 2.4 Mbit/s.
  \end{enumerate}
}

\answerframe{bit rate of two streams}{%
  \small
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item[a.] MJPEG: $24 \times 1024 \times 8 \times 10 = 1.97$ Mbit/s.\\
          H.264: $(40 + 29 \times 9) \times 1024 \times 8 = 301 \times 8192
          = 2.47$ Mbit/s.
    \item[b.] $331.8 / 2.47 = 135$. The MJPEG stream has a third of the
          frames and a smaller image, and it still needs 80 percent of the
          H.264 bit rate.
    \item[c.] One group: $1.97 + 2.47 = 4.43$ Mbit/s. 12 groups: 53.2
          Mbit/s. It does not fit. Options: fewer frames from the XIAO, a
          lower bit rate, a cable for each Raspberry Pi, or the 5 GHz band
          for the Raspberry Pi.
    \item[d.] $40 \times 1024 \times 8 / 2\,400\,000 = 0.137$ s: about 4
          frame times. Each keyframe adds this delay.
  \end{enumerate}
  \keypoint{Bit rate = bytes of a frame $\times$ 8 $\times$ frames each
    second. For H.264, add the keyframes and the P-frames of one GOP.}
}
