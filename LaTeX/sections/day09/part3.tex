\begin{frame}{How long does the battery supply the device?}
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.52\textwidth}r@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Keyword model on a Cortex-M33 board} & \textbf{Published} \\
    \midrule
    Latency of one inference   & 29.1 ms \\
    Energy of one inference    & 656.2 $\mu$J \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.5em
  \begin{itemize}
    \item A product makes one inference in each second. Its battery has
          1000 mAh at 3.7 V.
    \item The inferences alone use 0.66 mW. The battery has energy for 235
          days of inferences.
    \item No product gets this time. The device waits 971 ms in each second,
          and the wait also needs power.
    \item A board that waits with 157 mW empties the same battery in less
          than one day.
  \end{itemize}
  \keypoint{The latency says how long the processor works. The battery
    life depends on all 1000 ms of each second.}
  \source{MLPerf Tiny v1.2, board NUCLEO-U575ZI-Q (MLCommons).\\
    The latency and the battery values are calculations.}
\end{frame}

% --------------------------------------------------------------- definitions
\begin{frame}{Power, energy, and a battery}
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
  \begingroup\centering
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.16\textwidth}L{0.30\textwidth}L{0.44\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Quantity} & \textbf{Formula and unit} & \textbf{It tells you} \\
    \midrule
    Power    & $P = V \times I$, in W
             & How fast the device uses energy at this moment \\
    Energy   & $E = P \times t$, in J
             & What one task costs: one inference, one day \\
    Capacity & mAh
             & The charge of a battery. With its voltage: the energy. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}\par\endgroup
  \vskip 0.5em
  \begin{itemize}
    \item 1 J = 1 W for 1 s. 1 mW for 1 ms is 1 $\mu$J. 1 Wh = 3600 J.
    \item A battery with 1000 mAh at 3.7 V has
          $1 \,\text{Ah} \times 3.7 \,\text{V} = 3.7$ Wh. This is 13\,320 J.
    \item Battery life in hours = energy of the battery in Wh, divided by
          the mean power in W.
    \item 3.7 Wh and a mean power of 0.217 W give 17 hours.
  \end{itemize}
  \keypoint{A data sheet gives a current in mA. Multiply it by the supply
    voltage to get the power.}
\end{frame}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- CMOS power
\begin{frame}{Where the power of a chip goes}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{Two parts}
        \small
        $P = \alpha\, C\, V^2 f \;+\; I_{\text{leak}}\, V$\\[3pt]
        Switching: it grows with the clock $f$ and with the square of the
        voltage $V$.\\[2pt]
        Leakage: it is there with no work, and it grows with the
        temperature.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.50\textwidth}
      \footnotesize
      \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
      \setlength{\tabcolsep}{4pt}
      \begin{tabular}{@{}lrr@{}}
        \toprule
        \textbf{Accelerator} & \textbf{0.9 V} & \textbf{1.1 V} \\
        \midrule
        Clock          & 30.7 MHz & 98.7 MHz \\
        Latency        & 4.4 ms   & 1.5 ms \\
        Mean power     & 7.2 mW   & 29.6 mW \\
        Energy         & 31.5 $\mu$J & 43.8 $\mu$J \\
        \bottomrule
      \end{tabular}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 0.5em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The same chip of Part 1. With the higher voltage and the faster
          clock, it is 2.9 times as fast and uses 4.1 times the power.
    \item The energy of one inference is 39 percent higher. The higher
          voltage has a cost for each operation.
    \item A faster clock needs a higher voltage. So the power grows faster
          than the speed.
  \end{itemize}
  \keypoint{Speed costs power. A chip that runs slower at a lower voltage
    uses less energy for the same work.}
  \source{MLPerf Tiny v1.2, keyword model (MLCommons).\\
    The latency and the power are calculations.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A slower clock does not save energy}
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}rrrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Clock} & \textbf{Current} & \textbf{Time}
    & \textbf{Current $\times$ time} & \textbf{Factor} \\
    \midrule
    240 MHz & 65.9 mA & 1.0 $\times\, t$ & 65.9 $\times\, t$  & 1.00 \\
    160 MHz & 54.6 mA & 1.5 $\times\, t$ & 81.9 $\times\, t$  & 1.24 \\
    80 MHz  & 42.6 mA & 3.0 $\times\, t$ & 127.8 $\times\, t$ & 1.94 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.5em
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The table has one assumption: the time of an inference is
          proportional to 1 / clock. You measure this in the lab.
    \item The voltage is the same in each row, so the energy follows the
          current multiplied by the time.
    \item At 80 MHz the current is 35 percent lower. But the inference needs
          3 times as long, so its energy is almost 2 times as large.
    \item A large part of the current does not depend on the clock. The
          better plan: calculate at the full clock, then sleep.
  \end{itemize}
  \keypoint{Low power is not low energy. Compare the energy of the complete
    task.}
  \source{Calculations with the data sheet of the ESP32-S3.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Four ways to get a power value}
  \footnotesize
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.2}
  \begingroup\centering
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.24\textwidth}L{0.34\textwidth}L{0.34\textwidth}@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Method} & \textbf{It gives} & \textbf{Limit} \\
    \midrule
    Data sheet value
        & A typical current for each state of the chip
        & The chip only, and not your program \\
    USB power meter between the supply and the board
        & Voltage, current, and power of the complete board
        & Slow, and not exact enough for a sleep current \\
    Energy monitor, or a small resistor in the supply line with an
    oscilloscope
        & The current many times in each second: one inference is visible
        & More equipment and more work \\
    Sensors of the board (Raspberry Pi 5)
        & The voltage and the current of each supply rail
        & An estimate. Compare it with a meter one time. \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}\par\endgroup
  \vskip 0.4em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item MLPerf Tiny uses an energy monitor that supplies the device and
          measures the energy of the timed window.
    \item The lab uses a USB power meter if the classroom has it. If not:
          the data sheet for the kit, and the sensors for the Raspberry Pi.
  \end{itemize}
  \keypoint{Each method is valid if the report names it. A value with no
    method is not valid.}
  \source{MLPerf Tiny: Banbury et al., 2021.}
\end{frame}

% ----------------------------------------------------------- energy per inf.
\begin{frame}{Energy per inference: two methods}
  \begin{columns}[T]
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{From the power and the latency}
        \small
        $E = P_{\text{active}} \times t$\\[3pt]
        Read the power during a long run. Multiply it by the median
        latency.\\[3pt]
        Example: 217 mW and 50 ms give 10.85 mJ.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.48\textwidth}
      \begin{block}{From the energy of many inferences}
        \small
        $E = E_{\text{window}} \,/\, N$\\[3pt]
        Measure the energy of a window with $N$ inferences, and divide.\\[3pt]
        This is the method of MLPerf Tiny: 10 seconds or more for each
        window.
      \end{block}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 0.5em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The first method needs only a slow meter. The second method needs
          a meter that adds the energy.
    \item Decide what you do with the idle power. ``Total'': the complete
          power during the inference. ``Added'': the power above the idle
          state. Write which one you report.
    \item Use the same window as for the latency: the model only, or the
          pipeline.
    \item Warm up first. The power changes with the temperature.
  \end{itemize}
  \source{MLPerf Tiny: Banbury et al., 2021.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{A faster board is not always more efficient}
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.48\textwidth}rr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Power of the Raspberry Pi 5} & \textbf{Value}
    & \textbf{Divided by 217 mW} \\
    \midrule
    Published example with no load        & 3.2 W & 15 \\
    Moderate load, lower limit            & 5 W   & 23 \\
    Moderate load, upper limit            & 7 W   & 32 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.5em
  \begin{itemize}
    \item The same model runs on the two boards. The Raspberry Pi is much
          faster, and it uses much more power.
    \item With 5 W against 217 mW, the Raspberry Pi uses less energy for
          one inference only if it is more than 23 times as fast.
    \item For a small model, the fixed power of the large board is the
          problem: it pays 3 W also when it does nothing.
    \item In the lab you measure the speed factor for four models. Then you
          know the answer for each of them.
  \end{itemize}
  \keypoint{Energy per inference = power $\times$ latency. A board wins only
    if its speed gain is larger than its power factor.}
  \source{Raspberry Pi 5: \emph{Edge AI Engineering}, chapter ``Setup''.\\
    ESP32-S3: its data sheet.}
\end{frame}

% --------------------------------------------------------------- duty cycle
\begin{frame}{The duty cycle}
  \begingroup\centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\scriptsize, x=1cm, y=1cm]
    \draw[coolgray, ->, >={Stealth[length=4pt]}] (0,0) -- (10.3,0)
      node[below left] {time};
    \draw[coolgray, ->, >={Stealth[length=4pt]}] (0,0) -- (0,1.9);
    \node[coolgray, anchor=west] at (0.1,1.95) {power};
    \foreach \k in {0,1,2} {
      \fill[cardinalred!55] (0.3+\k*3.2,0) rectangle (0.3+\k*3.2+0.5,1.5);
      \fill[darkcyan!40] (0.8+\k*3.2,0) rectangle (3.5+\k*3.2,0.12);
    }
    \node[cardinalred] at (0.55,1.68) {active};
    \node[darkcyan] at (2.1,0.62) {sleep};
    \draw[coolgray] (0.3,-0.2) -- (0.3,-0.35) -- (3.5,-0.35) -- (3.5,-0.2);
    \node[coolgray, below] at (1.9,-0.35) {period $T$};
    \draw[coolgray] (3.5,1.68) -- (4.0,1.68);
    \node[coolgray, anchor=west] at (4.0,1.68) {$t$: time of the work in one
      period};
    \draw[cardinalred, dashed, thick] (0,0.34) -- (10.1,0.34);
    \node[cardinalred, anchor=west] at (7.2,0.52) {mean power};
  \end{tikzpicture}\par\endgroup
  \vskip 0.3em
  \small
  \[
    \alpha = \frac{t}{T}
    \qquad
    P_{\text{mean}} = \alpha\, P_{\text{active}} + (1 - \alpha)\, P_{\text{rest}}
  \]
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $\alpha$ is the duty cycle: the part of the time in which the
          device works.
    \item $P_{\text{rest}}$ is the power of the state between two tasks: the
          idle state, the light sleep, or the deep sleep.
    \item A camera with a frame rate has $\alpha$ near 1. A sensor that
          reports one value in each minute has $\alpha$ near 0.
  \end{itemize}
  \keypoint{The mean power, not the peak power, empties the battery.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The rest state decides the battery life}
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Duty cycle} & \textbf{Mean power} & \textbf{Battery life}
    & \textbf{Part of the sleep in the energy} \\
    \midrule
    100 percent  & 217 mW   & 17 hours  & 0 percent \\
    10 percent   & 22.4 mW  & 6.9 days  & 3 percent \\
    1 percent    & 2.95 mW  & 52 days   & 26 percent \\
    0.1 percent  & 1.01 mW  & 153 days  & 78 percent \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.5em

    \item Active power 217 mW, light sleep of the chip 0.79 mW, battery

    \item From 100 to 10 percent, the battery life grows by a factor of

    \item With the light sleep of the Sense board (19 mW) and a duty cycle

  \end{itemize}
  \keypoint{Find the larger term first: $\alpha\, P_{\text{active}}$ or

  \source{Calculations with the data sheet of the ESP32-S3\\

\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{The floor: energy times rate}
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.40\textwidth}rr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{Keyword model} & \textbf{Energy}
    & \textbf{One inference each second} \\
    \midrule
    Cortex-M4 at 120 MHz             & 1820.5 $\mu$J & 1.82 mW \\
    Cortex-M7 at 280 MHz             & 1375.6 $\mu$J & 1.38 mW \\
    Cortex-M33 at 160 MHz            & 656.2 $\mu$J  & 0.66 mW \\
    Neural accelerator at 0.9 V      & 31.5 $\mu$J   & 0.03 mW \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.5em
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Energy per inference $\times$ inferences in each second = the mean
          power of the inferences. 1 $\mu$J in each second is 1 $\mu$W.
    \item This is the lowest mean power that the device can have at this
          rate. The rest power comes on top.
    \item An always-on keyword detector makes about 4 inferences in each
          second. It also needs the microphone and the features all the
          time.
    \item So a product uses two stages (Day 5): a very small detector that
          runs all the time, and a larger model that starts on demand.
  \end{itemize}
  \source{MLPerf Tiny v1.2, closed division (MLCommons).\\
    The power is a calculation.}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Heat: why a chip becomes slower}
  \begin{columns}[c]
    \begin{column}{0.46\textwidth}
      \centering
      \begin{tikzpicture}[font=\scriptsize, x=0.40cm, y=0.05cm,
          >={Stealth[length=4pt]}]
        \draw[coolgray, ->] (0,40) -- (10.6,40) node[below left] {time};
        \draw[coolgray, ->] (0,40) -- (0,92);
        \foreach \v in {50,60,70,80} {
          \draw[coolgray] (0,\v) -- (-0.15,\v) node[left] {\v};
        }
        \node[coolgray, anchor=south] at (0,92) {$^\circ$C};
        \fill[cardinalred!12] (0,80) rectangle (10.2,85);
        \draw[cardinalred, dashed] (0,85) -- (10.2,85);
        \node[cardinalred, anchor=south east] at (10.2,85.5) {limit 85};
        \node[cardinalred, anchor=north east] at (10.2,79.5) {slower from 80};
        \draw[darkcyan, thick] (0,47) -- (1,47) .. controls (2.5,70) and
          (4,80) .. (5.5,82) -- (10,82);
        \draw[coolgray, dotted] (1,40) -- (1,36);
        \node[coolgray, anchor=north] at (1,36) {load starts};
      \end{tikzpicture}
    \end{column}
    \begin{column}{0.52\textwidth}
      \small
      \begin{itemize}
        \item All the electric power of a chip becomes heat. The temperature
              grows until the heat that leaves is equal to the power.
        \item Near its limit, the board decreases the clock of the
              processor: \textbf{thermal throttling}. The latency grows,
              with no change of the program.
      \end{itemize}
    \end{column}
  \end{columns}
  \vskip 0.3em
  \small
  \begin{itemize}
    \item Raspberry Pi: between 80 and 85 $^\circ$C, the clock of the cores
          goes down in steps. The limit is 85 $^\circ$C.
    \item The cooler of the Raspberry Pi 5 starts at 50 $^\circ$C and has
          its full speed at 75 $^\circ$C.
  \end{itemize}
  \keypoint{A hot chip is a slower chip. The temperature is a part of the
    system description.}
  \source{The limits: the documentation of the Raspberry Pi.}
\end{frame}

\exerciseframe{the battery life of a keyword detector}{%
  \small
  A keyword detector uses an ESP32-S3. For each window of audio it works
  for 50 ms: the features and the model. It has one window in each 250 ms.
  \vskip 0.3em
  {\footnotesize
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.1}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.46\textwidth}r@{}}
    \toprule
    Active power                              & 217 mW \\
    Power when the chip waits (idle)          & 157 mW \\
    Power in the light sleep                  & 0.79 mW \\
    Battery                                   & 1000 mAh at 3.7 V \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}}
  \vskip 0.2em
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Calculate the duty cycle.
    \item Calculate the mean power and the battery life if the chip waits
          between two windows.
    \item Do the same for the light sleep between two windows.
    \item The product must run for one month (720 hours). Which mean power
          is permitted? Which duty cycle gives it, with the light sleep?
    \item Name two changes that can give this duty cycle, and one power
          consumer that is not in the table.
  \end{enumerate}
}

\answerframe{the battery life of a keyword detector}{%
  \small
  \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.15}
  \begin{tabular}{@{}L{0.40\textwidth}rr@{}}
    \toprule
    \textbf{State between two windows} & \textbf{Mean power}
    & \textbf{Battery life} \\
    \midrule
    The chip waits   & 169.0 mW & 21.9 hours \\
    Light sleep      & 44.0 mW  & 84 hours: 3.5 days \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
  \vskip 0.3em
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item $50 / 250 = 0.2$: a duty cycle of 20 percent.
    \item $0.2 \times 217 + 0.8 \times 157 = 169.0$ mW. $3700 / 169.0 =
          21.9$ hours.
    \item $0.2 \times 217 + 0.8 \times 0.79 = 44.0$ mW. $3700 / 44.0 = 84$
          hours.
    \item $3700 / 720 = 5.14$ mW. $\alpha = (5.14 - 0.79) / (217 - 0.79) =
          0.020$: a duty cycle of 2 percent, or 5 ms of work in each 250 ms.
    \item Changes: a faster model (\code{int8}, a smaller network, better
          kernels); fewer windows; a small first stage that starts the model
          only when the sound is loud; an accelerator (0.03 mJ for one
          inference in Part 1). Not in the table: the microphone, the
          voltage regulator and the other parts of the board, and the
          radio.
  \end{enumerate}
}
